## 1. What is pneumonia?

Pneumonia is an infection of the lungs. The lungs are made of tiny air sacs called alveoli. In pneumonia, these air sacs fill with pus and fluid. This makes breathing painful and lowers the amount of oxygen the body can take in.

It can affect one lung or both. It can be mild, or it can be life-threatening, especially for babies, young children, older people and people with other illnesses.

**Sources:**
- WHO, Pneumonia fact sheet: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pneumonia
- CDC, About pneumonia: https://www.cdc.gov/pneumonia/about/index.html

## 2. Common causes

Many germs can cause pneumonia.

| Type | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (pneumococcus), *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib), *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Legionella*, whooping cough (pertussis) | In children, pneumococcus is the most common bacterial cause and Hib the second most common (WHO). |
| **Viruses** | Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza (flu), COVID-19, human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza, rhinovirus; less often measles, chickenpox, adenovirus | RSV is the most common viral cause in children (WHO). |
| **Fungi** | *Pneumocystis jirovecii* and others | Less common. Mostly affects people with a weak immune system, e.g., HIV-infected infants (WHO). |

Doctors cannot always find out which germ caused the infection (CDC).

**Sources:**
- WHO fact sheet: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pneumonia
- CDC, About pneumonia (causes): https://www.cdc.gov/pneumonia/about/index.html

## 3. Symptoms in adults

- Cough (may bring up yellow or green mucus/phlegm)
- Shortness of breath
- High temperature (fever) or chills
- Chest pain, often worse when breathing or coughing
- Body aches and feeling very tired
- Loss of appetite
- Wheezing
- Confusion — common in older people
- Some people also have nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea

**Sources:**
- NHS, Pneumonia: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pneumonia/
- CDC, About pneumonia (symptoms): https://www.cdc.gov/pneumonia/about/index.html

## 4. Symptoms and danger signs in children (WHO)

**Common signs:** cough and/or difficult breathing, with or without fever. Wheezing is more common when a virus is the cause.

**Fast breathing** — breaths per minute at or above these limits:

| Child's age | Fast breathing means |
|---|---|
| Under 2 months | 60 or more breaths per minute |
| 2 to 11 months | 50 or more breaths per minute |
| 1 to 5 years | 40 or more breaths per minute |

**Chest indrawing:** the lower chest wall pulls **in** when the child breathes in (instead of moving out).

**General danger signs — take the child to a hospital immediately:**
- Not able to drink or breastfeed
- Vomiting everything
- Convulsions (fits)
- Very sleepy, hard to wake, or unconscious
- Stridor (a harsh noise when breathing in) while the child is calm
- Severe malnutrition
- Very sick babies may also be unable to feed, have a low body temperature, or become unconscious.

WHO classifies a child with fast breathing and/or chest indrawing as **pneumonia** (usually treated with oral antibiotics and home care advice by a health worker), and a child with any general danger sign as **severe pneumonia** (needs referral and injectable antibiotics).

**Sources:**
- WHO fact sheet: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pneumonia
- WHO (2014), *Revised WHO classification and treatment of childhood pneumonia at health facilities — Quick reference guide*: https://www.childhealthtaskforce.org/sites/default/files/2019-06/Advocacy%20Package_Pneumonia%20Quick%20Reference%20Guide%28WHO%2C%202014%29_Thumbnail.pdf

## 5. Emergency warning signs — go to hospital now

Call an ambulance (India: **108**, or **112**) or go to the nearest emergency department **immediately** if:

- You are struggling to breathe — choking, gasping, unable to speak
- Skin, lips or tongue look pale, blue or blotchy
- You cough up blood
- You suddenly become confused
- A baby cannot be woken, or feels floppy
- A child shows any WHO danger sign (section 4)

Do not drive yourself. Ask someone to drive you, or call an ambulance.

**See a doctor urgently (same day)** if you have a cough with yellow or green phlegm, chest pain that comes and goes, feel short of breath, or are not better 2–3 days after starting antibiotics.

**Sources:**
- NHS, Pneumonia (urgent advice): https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pneumonia/
- WHO 2014 quick reference guide (child danger signs): see section 4
- India ambulance numbers: Parliament of India replies list 108 and 102 as the National Health Mission ambulance numbers (e.g., https://rsdebate.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/757940/1/PQ_267_04022025_U296_p534_p537.pdf); 112 is the national emergency number. Local practice varies by state.

## 6. Who is at higher risk?

- **Young children** — under 5 years, especially babies
- **Older adults** — 65 years and over
- People with **long-term illnesses** — heart disease, long-term lung disease, diabetes, liver disease
- People with a **weak immune system** (for example, HIV) — highest risk
- People who **smoke**, or drink **too much alcohol**
- People who are **often around sick people**
- For children (WHO): **malnutrition**, not being exclusively breastfed, **measles** or **HIV**, **smoke from cooking or heating** with wood or dung, **crowded homes**, and **parents who smoke**

**Sources:**
- CDC, Risk factors: https://www.cdc.gov/pneumonia/risk-factors/index.html
- NHS, Pneumonia: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pneumonia/
- WHO fact sheet: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pneumonia

## 7. Prevention

**Vaccines** (ask your doctor which ones you need):
- **Pneumococcal vaccine** — for babies, adults over 65 and people at higher risk
- **Hib vaccine** — for babies and young children
- **Influenza (flu)** vaccine
- **Measles** vaccine
- **Pertussis (whooping cough)** vaccine
- Also: COVID-19, RSV and chickenpox (varicella) vaccines or antibody products, where offered

WHO says immunisation against Hib, pneumococcus, measles and whooping cough is the most effective way to prevent pneumonia in children.

**Everyday habits:**
- Wash hands often with soap and water
- Cough or sneeze into a tissue or your elbow
- Do not smoke, and keep children away from cigarette smoke
- Use cleaner cooking stoves to reduce smoke inside the home
- Breastfeed babies **only** breast milk for the first 6 months, and give children good nutrition
- Keep long-term conditions such as asthma and diabetes well controlled
- Avoid close contact with people who are sick

**Sources:**
- WHO fact sheet (prevention): https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pneumonia
- CDC, Prevention: https://www.cdc.gov/pneumonia/prevention/index.html
- NHS, Pneumonia (vaccines): https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pneumonia/

## 8. How doctors diagnose pneumonia

1. **History** — symptoms, when they started, vaccines, smoking, contact with sick people, travel.
2. **Physical exam** — temperature, breathing rate, and listening to the lungs with a stethoscope.
3. **Chest X-ray** — looks for inflammation in the lungs. Often used to confirm pneumonia.
4. **Pulse oximetry** — a small clip on the finger or ear measures oxygen in the blood.
5. **Blood tests** — e.g., complete blood count to look for infection; blood culture to find the germ.
6. **Other tests when needed** — sputum (phlegm) test, PCR test, arterial blood gas (very sick patients), chest CT scan, bronchoscopy, or testing fluid around the lung.

**Sources:**
- NHLBI (US National Institutes of Health), Pneumonia — Diagnosis: https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/pneumonia/diagnosis
- NHS, Pneumonia: https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pneumonia/

## 9. Care at home (always follow your doctor's advice)

- **Rest** and drink **plenty of fluids**.
- Take paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever or pain **only if your doctor or pharmacist says you can**.
- If you are given antibiotics, **take the full course**, even if you feel better.
- **Do not** take cough medicine unless a doctor advises it — coughing helps clear the infection.
- Cover coughs and sneezes, wash hands, and do not smoke.
- Contact your doctor if you are **not better after 2–3 days** of antibiotics, or **any time** you feel worse. Go to hospital for any sign in section 5.

**Sources:**
- NHS, Pneumonia (treatment and self-care): https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/pneumonia/

## 11. For doctors: CURB-65 severity score (adults with community-acquired pneumonia)

Score **1 point** for each:

| Letter | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **C** | **Confusion** — Mental Test Score of 8 or less, or new disorientation in person, place or time |
| **U** | **Urea > 7 mmol/L** (BUN > 19 mg/dL) |
| **R** | **Respiratory rate ≥ 30 /min** |
| **B** | **Blood pressure**: systolic < 90 mmHg **or** diastolic ≤ 60 mmHg |
| **65** | **Age ≥ 65 years** |

Total 0–5.

**30-day mortality by score** (Lim et al. 2003, Table 4; derivation cohort n = 718 / validation cohort n = 214):

| Score | Derivation: died / total (%) | Validation: died / total (%) | Recommended care (Lim et al., Fig 2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1/137 (0.7%) | 0/36 (0%) | Likely suitable for home treatment |
| 1 | 4/187 (2.1%) | 0/54 (0%) | Likely suitable for home treatment |
| 2 | 17/184 (9.2%) | 5/60 (8.3%) | Consider hospital-supervised treatment (short-stay inpatient or hospital-supervised outpatient) |
| 3 | 20/138 (14.5%) | 9/42 (21.4%) | Manage in hospital as severe pneumonia |
| 4 | 26/65 (40%) | 5/19 (26.3%) | Manage in hospital as severe pneumonia; **assess for ICU** |
| 5 | 1/7 (14%)* | 1/3 (33.3%) | Manage in hospital as severe pneumonia; **assess for ICU** |

\*Only 7 patients scored 5; 1 died and 3 others needed mechanical ventilation in intensive care and survived (Lim et al.). The small number explains the odd percentage.
Note: the paper's abstract quotes slightly different per-score figures; the table above uses the paper's Table 4.

**By risk group** (Lim et al. 2003, Figure 2, derivation cohort):

| Group | Score | 30-day mortality | Care |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Low | 0–1 | **1.5%** (5/324) | Likely suitable for home treatment |
| 2 — Intermediate | 2 | **9.2%** (17/184) | Consider hospital-supervised treatment |
| 3 — High | 3–5 | **22%** (47/210) | Manage in hospital as severe pneumonia; assess for ICU especially if score 4–5 |

**NICE risk bands (UK guideline):** CURB65 0–1 low risk (< 3% mortality); 2 intermediate (3–15%); 3–5 high (> 15%). Always use clinical judgement alongside the score.

### CRB-65 (when blood urea is not available, e.g., in a clinic)

Same as CURB-65 but **without urea** (score 0–4).

| Group | CRB-65 | 30-day mortality (Lim et al., Fig 3) | Care (Lim et al.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Low | 0 | **1.2%** (2/167) | Likely suitable for home treatment |
| 2 — Intermediate | 1–2 | **8.15%** (37/455) | Likely need hospital referral and assessment |
| 3 — High | 3–4 | **31%** (30/96) | Urgent hospital admission |

**NICE risk bands for CRB65 (primary care):** 0 low risk (< 1% mortality); 1–2 intermediate (1–10%); 3–4 high (> 10%).

**App note:** CURB-65 is for **adults** with pneumonia. Do not use it for children; for children use the WHO signs in section 4.

**Sources:**
- Lim WS, van der Eerden MM, Laing R, et al. Defining community acquired pneumonia severity on presentation to hospital: an international derivation and validation study. *Thorax* 2003;58(5):377–382. https://doi.org/10.1136/thorax.58.5.377 (PubMed 12728155; PMC1746657)

## 12. For doctors and students: how pneumonia looks on a chest X-ray

- **Consolidation** — a white (opaque) area where air in the alveoli has been replaced by fluid or other material, such as pus in bacterial pneumonia. It hides the blood vessels and airway walls inside it. When it spreads through a whole lobe it is called lobar pneumonia.
- **Air bronchograms** — dark, branching air-filled airways seen **inside** the white area. They are more typical of consolidation than of lung collapse.
- **Silhouette sign** — when a white area touches the heart or diaphragm, the normal sharp edge disappears. This helps locate the lobe:
  - right heart border lost → right middle lobe
  - left heart border lost → lingula (left upper lobe)
  - a hemidiaphragm lost → the lower lobe on that side
- **Pleural effusion** — fluid in the space around the lung. The WHO standard for reading children's X-rays counts "alveolar consolidation or pleural effusion" as primary end-point pneumonia.
- **Interstitial / patchy pattern** — streaky or patchy shadows in both lungs; viral pneumonia often shows a more diffuse "interstitial" pattern (as described in Hashmi et al. 2020).
- **Remember:** a chest X-ray cannot tell which germ caused the pneumonia. Other conditions (heart failure, bleeding, some cancers) can also cause consolidation. Always read the X-ray with the clinical picture.
